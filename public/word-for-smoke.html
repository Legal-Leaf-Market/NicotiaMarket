Language & scripture

the word for smokeis the word for soul

Everything is vapor. Including you.

Our name comes from a word that is 190 years old. This one is older. In the Hebrew of the oldest book most of us have in the house, breath, wind, spirit and soul are not four ideas politely sharing a metaphor. They are very nearly the same small set of words. And the word that book uses for a fleeting exhalation is the same word it uses for everything that exists. Once you see it you cannot stop seeing it.

TL;DR, for anyone who came here to buy pouches

  • In Biblical Hebrew the word for soul is built out of the word for throat. They put the self at the bit where the air goes through.
  • Hevel, the word Ecclesiastes repeats until it becomes a drumbeat, has been translated "vanity" since 1611. It actually means vapor.
  • In Exodus, God knocks off work and, in the Hebrew, catches his breath.
  • The first thing scripture ever calls holy is not a temple or a mountain. It is a day off.
  • Abel, in Hebrew, is that same word for vapor. The first person in the book to die is called Breath.

0

13.8 billion years ago

Start before the plant

Nicotine is C10H14N2. The fourteen hydrogens in it were made in the first minutes of the universe and essentially none has been made since. The carbon and the nitrogen could not exist yet, because nothing back then could build them. They had to be cooked inside stars, and those stars had to die and throw the results outward, before there was an Earth for any of it to land on.

The word is 190 years old. The molecule is around a hundred million. The atoms are 13.8 billion.

I

day seven

God took a smoke break

We are going to say it out loud, because it is the joke everyone makes and it is sitting on top of something real.

Genesis 2:2: on the seventh day the work is finished and God שָׁבַת, shavat, ceased. That is the root Sabbath comes from, and it does not mean "was tired". It means the work came to an end.

Now Exodus 31:17, where the same six-days-then-one gets repeated and the Hebrew adds a second verb that most English Bibles flatten into "and was refreshed". The word is וַיִּנָּפַשׁ, vayinafash, and it is built from נֶפֶשׁ, nefesh, the word for soul. Robert Alter, who spent decades getting this into English that keeps its edges, renders the line:

"God rested and caught His breath."

Six days of work, then the maker of everything steps outside for a minute. And if we are made in the image, the break is not a lapse in the work. It is part of the picture we were copied from.

Believe as much or as little of it as you like. As a piece of writing, the creation account ends with the boss going outside, which is a very good ending.

II

the first holy thing

The oldest sacred object we have is a pause

Genesis 2:3 does something people miss because they are busy reading the six days. It is the first time the word קָדוֹשׁ (kadosh, holy) appears in scripture at all. Not attached to a mountain, a temple, an altar or a person. Those all come later. The first holy thing is a day.

Abraham Joshua Heschel built a whole book on that in 1951. His phrase is a palace in time, the point being that a tradition famous for arguing about one piece of real estate opens by sanctifying the one thing nobody can own or fence you out of. You cannot buy a Tuesday.

The first holy thing in the book is not a place. It is a break.

Which is a funny thing to have been carrying around while the twentieth century argued about whether workers could have fifteen minutes outside. That argument has its own page →

III

the vocabulary

Soul, wind, breath, spirit: pick one, it will not help

Biblical Hebrew does not keep our tidy line between the physical act of breathing and the invisible animating self. It mostly has these:

  1. רוּחַ  ruachWind. Breath. Spirit. Same word for all three, and it turns up in the second sentence of the Bible as something moving over the water that is either a wind or a spirit depending entirely on your translator's mood.
  2. נְשָׁמָה  neshamahBreath. In Genesis 2:7 the human is dust until God breathes into the nostrils, and it is that exhalation that turns a shape into a someone.
  3. נֶפֶשׁ  nefeshTranslated "soul" for four hundred years. At the root it means throat, so it also carries breath, appetite, hunger, the whole living want of a body.
  4. הֶבֶל  hevelVapor. Mist. The visible cloud of a breath on a cold morning. There, and then not there.

Sit with nefesh a second. The word a whole civilisation used for the soul is, underneath, the name of the part of you that air moves through. Not the heart. Not the head. The throat. They located the self at the place where the outside comes in.

Which is why vayinafash is so good. It is not "God had a nap". It is nearer to God re-souled himself, a verb made out of the throat, meaning the thing that happens when you finally stop and take one deep one.

There is a fifth, and we are handing it over carefully. Rabbi Arthur Waskow argues that the four letters of the unpronounceable name (יהוה) are all aspirate, so trying to say them with no vowels produces no word at all. You just breathe. Beautiful, widely loved, and not linguistic consensus: we genuinely do not know how the name was vocalised, precisely because it stopped being said. Take it as a lovely thing some people believe, which is how Waskow offers it. The rest of this chapter is dictionary work. This bit is not.

IV

the punchline

Everything is vapor

Ecclesiastes opens with a line everyone knows in a translation that has been quietly misleading them since 1611: Vanity of vanities, all is vanity.

The Hebrew is הֲבֵל הֲבָלִים, havel havalim, and hevel does not mean vanity. Not conceit, not pride, not pointlessness. It means vapor. The word lands five times in that one verse. Alter goes almost exactly here: merest breath, everything is mere breath.

So the most quoted pessimism in Western literature is not saying none of this matters. It is saying something stranger and much kinder: everything is a vapor. Your work, your money, your name, the thing you are worried about tonight. All of it has the density of a breath on a cold morning. Visible for a second, then gone. That is not an insult, it is a description, and it is the same description for everybody.

Everything is vapor. Including you. Including whoever is annoying you.

Then the detail that makes the hair stand up. Adam and Eve's second son is called הֶבֶל, Hevel. Identical word. We say it in English as Abel.

The first person in the story to die is named Vapor. He arrives, he is described as good, he is gone inside a few verses. Somebody built that on purpose, thousands of years ago, then dropped the same word two hundred times into a book about how briefly everything lasts.

V

so, us

Meet me outside

The oldest vocabulary humans have for the self is a vocabulary of breath. The first thing it calls holy is a break. And what a person does on a break, in every century we have records for, is step outside, away from the work, and deliberately move something through the throat. Air. Smoke. Vapor. Steam off a coffee. The mechanism has changed a dozen times. The gesture has not moved an inch.

We did not invent the break. We are extremely late to it.

Worth saying too: for most of human history smoke was how you talked to whatever was above you. Not a side effect of worship, the medium of it. Burnt offerings and temple incense, the censer down a cathedral aisle, incense across Buddhist, Hindu, Shinto and Taoist practice. Psalm 141 asks for a prayer to rise like incense. The logic needs no theology: you cannot see a prayer, you can see smoke, and it goes up.

Smoke was the visible part of the prayer.

And it was never only this plant. In 2019 a team publishing in Science Advances found cannabinol residue in ten wooden braziers at a cemetery in the Pamirs dated to about 500 BCE. Potent cannabis, burned at a graveside. Which is the thread tying our three shops together: Nicotia is nicotine, Legal Leaf is the other thing humanity has been putting in the air for thousands of years, and Herbal Leaf is everything people reach for when it is time to put the first two down, which is the closest we come to quitting. Three storefronts, one very old habit: put a plant in the air, and breathe it. Most cultures did it at a funeral long before they did it for fun.

One boundary, plainly: ceremonial tobacco is a living sacred practice in many Indigenous nations, it is not the same thing as the commercial product this site compares prices for, and it is not ours to borrow as a marketing line. A pouch of Zyn is not a pipe.

VI

the black sheep bit

The smoking area is where they put you

In 2014 Michael Gungor, then one of the most successful worship musicians alive, published a post saying he could no longer read Genesis literally. The response was industrial. A Baptist church cancelled a concert, a Christian radio station pulled its support, three shows went in short order, and Ken Ham took him apart in public. A Grammy-nominated worship band got walked out of the Christian music business for saying out loud what plenty of people in the pews already quietly thought.

He asked the question everyone was thinking and got sent outside.

By his wife Lisa's own public account he went fully atheist in 2012, and has since described the return not as finding God again but as losing the machinery around God. What he kept is a question worth the whole ordeal: can a myth be beautiful, important, and even true?

Every church, school and office has the same geography. There is the room where everyone agrees, and there is a spot round the side where the people who asked something awkward end up standing together. Historically that spot has an ashtray in it. It has better conversations in it too, and everybody knows it. There is a saying that goes around American evangelical circles with no author attached, which is usually the sign a thing is true enough to survive without one:

"If there aren't cigarette butts in your parking lot, you're not doing church right."

Sit with what that claims. The litter is the evidence. A spotless car park means you have quietly filtered for people who already had it together, and any congregation doing the unglamorous work of taking in addicts, ex-cons and the barely holding on will always have a mess by the door. It is the most generous thing anyone has said about a cigarette end, and it was said by people who mostly do not approve of smoking.

The part we keep returning to, though, is that he put it down and then picked it back up, and narrated both halves while they were happening. Everyone who has used nicotine knows that shape. Quit in January, out the back door by March, six months clean and then a wedding. It is the most ordinary story in this industry and nobody tells it honestly, because both available scripts are lies: the one where you triumphantly never again, and the one where you were never really trying.

Picked up, put down, picked up again. That is not a character flaw. That is the shape of a long relationship with anything.

We are not telling anyone to pick anything back up. Nicotine is addictive, we sell nothing, we are not a quit aid, and if you are trying to stop then a doctor is the resource and we are not. What we will not do is the shame. That has been tried at industrial scale for sixty years on people standing outside in the rain, and the only thing it reliably produces is people lying about it.

VII

2010, then 2015

Dust, then breath

We did not go looking for this. It fell out of the dates.

Gungor's biggest song by a distance, sung in churches that would later refuse to book him, is Beautiful Things from 2010. Its central image is dust: dead ground, spent earth, the used-up stuff at the bottom, made into something worth looking at. That is the song from before, written from inside the building at the height of it.

Then the questions, the cancellations, and a year of straightforward atheism. And the song from after, reported to be the first he wrote to God on the way back, is Vapor. Which is hevel. Which is breath.

He wrote the dust song. He lost everything. He came back and wrote the breath song.

Now read Genesis 2:7 again, the verse we quoted four chapters ago without seeing where it was going. God takes the dust of the ground. God breathes into it. The result is a living nefesh, a soul. Dust plus breath is the entire recipe. Not dust. Not breath. Both, in that order, with a long unpleasant gap in between where it is only ever dirt.

And here is the part that will not leave us alone. Beautiful Things is still sung every Sunday, in enormous rooms, by people who love it, and they are right to. It is a song about God picking up what everybody else wrote off. Sing that at eleven, step into the car park at ten past, and find somebody against the wall having a cigarette because it is the only way they can get through the service. That is the person in the song. A great many rooms that sing it every week would quietly prefer that person went somewhere else.

They kept the song and lost the man who wrote it, and most of them will never hear how the story ends.

Which is not even a modern complaint. It is the complaint. The recurring, on-the-record objection to Jesus was not his theology, it was who he ate with: tax collectors, drunks, prostitutes, the generally disreputable. In Matthew 11:19 the accusation is quoted and not denied. In Matthew 21:31 he tells the most respectable men in the room that the tax collectors and prostitutes are getting in ahead of them. And in Matthew 7 the people turned away at the end are not the disreputable at all, they are the accomplished, arriving with a CV.

Jesus hung out with the smokers.

Not literally, obviously. The plant was on a continent nobody in that story had heard of. But he stepped outside constantly and the gospels keep bothering to mention it: up a mountain alone, out to a lonely place before light, away from the crowd, in the middle of the most urgent work in the book. Luke 5:16 puts it flatly. Whatever else is going on there, the plain reading is that the man left the room a lot, and the people writing it down thought that was worth recording rather than apologising for.

We are aware we are a nicotine price comparison site and that this is not, on paper, our department. We are also aware we would be lying if we said we noticed all this and felt nothing.

This is the song. It takes the hevel of Ecclesiastes directly and does the thing we have just spent seven chapters doing, in about four minutes, better. If you take one thing from this page, take this and not us.

What this is not

Not a claim that any scripture endorses nicotine. It does not. The plant was on another continent and unknown to every author quoted here. Nothing above is an argument that anyone should use anything.

Not a health claim. Nicotine is addictive. No etymology, cosmology or verse changes that, softens it, or is offered as a reason to start. We make no comparative safety claims and we are not a quit aid.

Not theology, and we are not the ones to do theology. This is a reading: of etymology, of translation choices, and of one very good joke about the seventh day. Millions hold the Sabbath as something serious and observed, not as a bit. That reading is theirs, and it is older and better founded than ours. If the joke does not land for you, take the Hebrew and leave it. The Hebrew is the good part anyway.

Not speaking for anyone named here. Michael and Lisa Gungor are living people who have never heard of us. Everything in Chapter VI and VII comes from contemporaneous reporting or their own public statements, the atheism is in the past tense because that is how they describe it, and none of it means they would endorse a nicotine market. We are admirers with a link.

And the etymology is unrelated to our name. Nicotia comes from Jean Nicot in the 1500s. Hevel and nefesh are thousands of years older and from a different language family. We are not claiming a link. We are pointing out that two separate traditions, with no contact, both ended up naming this thing after breath.

Where this comes from

Hebrew text and verse references are from the Masoretic text as presented in standard interlinear editions. The rendering of Exodus 31:17 as "God rested and caught His breath" is Robert Alter's, from his translation of the Five Books of Moses (W. W. Norton, 2004); the observation that vayinafash is built on the root of nefesh, and that nefesh is rooted in the throat, is standard lexicography rather than anything we came up with. The point about Genesis 2:3 containing the first appearance of kadosh in scripture, and the phrase palace in time, are Abraham Joshua Heschel's, from The Sabbath (1951). Reading hevel as vapor rather than vanity is the mainstream scholarly position and is reflected in most translations produced since the 1980s.

The Jirzankal Cemetery findings in Chapter V are from Ren et al., "The origins of cannabis smoking: chemical residue evidence from the first millennium BCE in the Pamirs", Science Advances (12 June 2019), ten wooden braziers, heated stones, cannabinol residue, in a mortuary context dated to around 500 BCE. It is one of the earliest directly dated and chemically verified instances of cannabis being burned and inhaled anywhere, and the funerary setting is the paper's own finding rather than our gloss on it.

The reading of the Tetragrammaton as breath is Rabbi Arthur Waskow's, argued over many years and in many places. We have flagged it in the body as a devotional reading rather than a linguistic finding, and we mean that: it is contested, the vocalisation of the name is genuinely not known, and it is repeated far more often than it is sourced. It is on this page because it is beautiful and because Waskow offers it honestly, not because we can prove it. The parking-lot line in Chapter VI circulates widely in American evangelical settings with no reliable attribution, so we have not invented one for it.

Gungor's Vapor appears on One Wild Life: Soul (2015). We reached this material through The Liturgists' body of work on breath and impermanence, which is where a great many people first met this reading of Ecclesiastes. We have credited them generally rather than citing a specific episode, because we could not verify an episode number and would rather under-claim than invent one.

The 2014 account in Chapter VI is drawn from contemporaneous coverage of the What Do We Believe? post and its fallout, the cancelled Baptist church concert, the Wisconsin radio station withdrawing, the three cancellations, and Ken Ham's public response, together with Michael Gungor's follow-up remarks on the Liturgists podcast. The account of his atheism is Lisa Gungor's, given to the Christian Post and to Relevant; the "myth can be beautiful, important and even true" framing is Michael Gungor's own, from his BioLogos interview. A widely repeated version of this story also claims band members were criticised for smoking. We could not source that anywhere and have deliberately left it out. It is exactly the detail this page would most like to be true, which is the best possible reason to hold it to a higher standard rather than a lower one.

Beautiful Things is the title track of the 2010 album of the same name and is comfortably Gungor's most-played song; the dust-and-dead-ground imagery described in Chapter VII is the song's own central figure. No lyrics from it, from Vapor, or from anything else are reproduced on this page. The songs are described and linked, never quoted, because they are not ours.

The gospel references in Chapter VII are Matthew 11:19 (the "friend of tax collectors and sinners" accusation, quoted and not denied), Matthew 21:31 (tax collectors and prostitutes entering ahead of the respectable), Matthew 7:22–23 ("I never knew you", addressed to the religiously accomplished), Matthew 25:31–46 (the criterion being feeding, visiting and finding people), Luke 5:16 (withdrawing to solitary places, and doing so habitually) and Hebrews 13:12–13 (suffering outside the gate, followed by the instruction to go out there).
