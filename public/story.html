Our story

the word beforethe word

Before it was nicotine, it was Nicotia — a name for the plant that poets used and chemists borrowed. It has been picked up and put down for four hundred years. We picked it up again.

I

before 1492

The plant had a hundred names, and none of them were ours

It grew in the Americas for thousands of years before Europe had a word for it. It was smoked, chewed, snuffed and burned as offering, and the people who grew it had their own names for it long before anyone wrote those names down.

The word Europe kept was tabaco — taken from an Arawakan language of the Caribbean, though what it originally named is still argued over. The rolled leaves, say some. The pipe it was drawn through, say others. What is not in doubt is the direction of travel: the plant was already ancient, and the naming was done by the newcomers.

Europe would spend the next century trying on names it liked better — petun, from Tupi; herba santa; herbe à tous les maux, the herb for all ills. Almost none of them stuck.

II

1560 · Lisbon and Paris

An ambassador sends a package

Jean Nicot de Villemain was France's ambassador to Portugal. In Lisbon he met the plant, believed what he was told about it, and sent seed and powdered leaf home to the French court — to Catherine de Médicis, with instructions for preparing it against her son's headaches.

The court was delighted. The queen is said to have named it Herba Regina, the queen's herb, and in France it went by herbe à la Reine and herbe de l'Ambassadeur for years afterwards. A diplomat's parcel had become a fashion.

Nicot did not discover tobacco. He did not grow it, and he was not the first European to send it anywhere. He wrote a dictionary and did his job. But his name was the one attached to the package, and names attach to whoever is holding the parcel when it lands.

III

1753 · Uppsala

Linnaeus makes it permanent

Nearly two centuries later, Carl Linnaeus was rebuilding the naming of every living thing. When he came to tobacco he reached back to the ambassador and fixed the genus as Nicotiana.

That is the moment the accident becomes permanent. A man who forwarded a parcel in 1560 is now welded to a genus, in Latin, in the scientific record. Every tobacco plant on earth carries the ambassador's name whether or not anyone remembers who he was.

Nicotiana tabacum. The Frenchman and the Taíno word, in the same breath, for ever.

IV

the 1830s onward

Nicotia — the name we took

Then the word slipped out of the herbarium and into ordinary speech. Shortened from the Latin, Nicotia became a common and poetic English name for the plant itself. The Oxford English Dictionary places its earliest known use in the 1830s.

Writers took to her. The American poet James Russell Lowell went so far as to make her a goddess — Nicotia, “dearer to the Muse / Than all the grape's bewildering juice.” By the end of the century there was a whole anthology of tobacco verse called Lyra Nicotiana.

This is the part that matters to us. For most of the nineteenth century, Nicotia was simply what you called it — not a brand, not a compound, not a warning label. A word ordinary people used for a thing ordinary people used.

V

1809 – 1893 · Paris and Heidelberg

The word goes into a laboratory and comes out a molecule

In 1809 the French chemist Louis Nicolas Vauquelin found something in tobacco he could not name. The something got a provisional name built, again, on the ambassador: nicotianine.

In 1828, at the University of Heidelberg, Wilhelm Posselt and Karl Reimann isolated it pure. They renamed what they had “Nikotin”. Melsens gave it a formula in 1843 — C10H14N2 — and Adolf Pinner worked out its structure in the 1890s.

So the sequence runs: a man, then a plant, then a poem, then a molecule. Nicot → Nicotiana → Nicotia → nicotine. Nicotia is the step in the middle — the last time the word belonged to people rather than to botany or chemistry, and one letter away from the name we all ended up using.

Nobody decided this. Four hundred years of people reaching for the same root because it was the one already lying there. The word keeps getting called back to.

now

And now we're calling it back
